THE EXPERIENCE PRINCIPLE

The physical environment is part of the interface. The screen should know when to step aside.

The design story

An interface that knows when to disappear.

Past Here is a concept for exploring local heritage through short, place-based stories. It asks a simple question: can a digital experience help people notice more of the city, while asking less of their screen?

01 · Starting point

From coursework to a focused experience

The starting point was University of Leicester CO7441 team coursework. Our group submission, Leicester Heritage Quest, explored creative, data-centred ways to connect students with Leicester’s heritage and culture. Time Travelers Trials was the name of one challenge within the wider concept.

I developed that shared starting point into Past Here: an individual place-based concept for discovering stories in Leicester’s streets. The single interactive prototype is a phone-sized story journey through real Leicester places, from a simulated nearby notification to a second stop.

02 · Team coursework

What our group proposed

The group designed for a digitally confident, first-year student who was new to Leicester and wanted accessible, social ways to connect with local heritage. That audience and the supporting research belong to the team submission; they are not research I conducted for this individual case study.

The concept sketch, journey map and technical diagram proposed location-based trails, photo-matching and knowledge challenges, AR story layers, points, badges, leaderboards and a community feed. The shared aim was to make exploration feel active and meaningful, while supporting belonging and cultural connection.

Team design qualities: emotional connection · active engagement · social relevance · inclusive access · cultural continuity.

These are group concepts and intended outcomes, not validated product features or measured results.

03 · Group journey map

Five stages, from noticing to returning

Our user journey mapped the experience as Awareness → Discovery → Engagement → Reflection → Continuation. It surfaced a different concern at each stage: notification fatigue, competing city distractions, getting stuck in a challenge, hesitation to share, and the novelty fading over time.

Awareness→Discovery→Engagement→Reflection→Continuation

The map suggested personalized nearby prompts, travel information, hints or collaboration, safer community moderation and relevant return prompts. It is a team artefact; the screen-to-street flow below is my individual response to that app-heavy engagement model.

04 · Individual design move

Keep the place-based story; change the rhythm

The team designed for first-year students and used points, leaderboards and a community feed for social connection. Past Here is for anyone walking through Leicester, whether they live here, are visiting or are studying. I kept the community voice, but moved it from in-app activity to attributed local memories that can sit beside an official record. I chose not to carry points or leaderboards into this prototype because I want the place and its stories to hold attention. The prototype shows the sourced record; it does not yet include a collected community memory. Next, I would test whether a lighter, optional way to collect stories helps people notice and revisit them, or distracts from the street.

Museum mobile-guide research records visitors’ concern that handheld devices could interfere with exhibit play and attention to companions, a risk often described as “heads-down” use [1]. A study on teenage museum experiences also cites evidence that short time limits can make young visitors hurry through exhibits to complete tasks [2]. Those findings made me question challenge-led engagement and test a shorter, optional interaction. This is a design hypothesis, not a finding from my own research.

“How might a digital heritage experience help people look up from their phones and notice the stories attached to the places around them?”

This is my design question. The phone-down direction is my individual response to the team journey, not a feature claimed by the original group submission.

05 · Experience rhythm

Screen → street → screen → street

The phone begins with a simulated notification for Jewry Wall. Tapping it opens Google Street View of the real site; below that, a period selector changes a separate image and the story text. The story scrolls inside the phone, then offers a nearby Leicester Guildhall stop, about five minutes away on foot, or a community-memory preview. I kept Street View and historic imagery in separate panels, with Google attribution intact. The notification and walking time are simulated; the prototype does not request location access or track a route. Outdoor heritage AR research reports design challenges involving interaction, surroundings, learner needs, context and technology [3]. A Kyoto study found smartphone comparisons between archival photographs and the present townscape helped people understand change [4]. That study used archive photos; here the Roman, Tudor and Victorian visuals are illustrative reconstructions, while the Edwardian view is an archival postcard.

Notice nearby cue→Look around→Compare a story→Walk to the next place

The present-day Street View remains Google’s own view; period imagery and source notes appear separately, with no historical image laid over it.

Interactive phone prototype

A nearby story, then the next stop

Open the simulated notification, look around Jewry Wall in Google Street View, then choose Roman, Tudor, Victorian or Edwardian to reveal a separate image and story. Scroll inside the phone to continue to Leicester Guildhall, about five minutes away on foot, or preview a community memory. The phone does not use live location, save posts or place artwork over Google’s imagery.

Prototype note: Roman, Tudor and Victorian images are AI-generated illustrative reconstructions, not archival evidence. The Edwardian view is an archival postcard with a source link. The Guildhall route time is an estimate; community writing is preview-only.

Use the controls inside the phone to open the notification, compare periods, scroll the story and follow the next-stop journey.

06 · Story & trust

Show where a story comes from

The Jewry Wall and Guildhall stories in this phone prototype link to their sources. The Roman, Tudor and Victorian period scenes are marked as illustrative reconstructions; the Edwardian postcard is archival. A community post can be previewed in the phone, but is not saved or published. A future version could place consented, attributed local accounts beside an official record, with review before publication.

Past Here treats heritage storytelling as interpretation rather than a single unquestioned voice. A future design iteration could test how people understand source labels and conflicting accounts.

07 · Inclusive options

More than one way to explore

The prototype offers a text-first story path and avoids relying on audio or motion to convey meaning. The page responds to reduced-motion preferences. Further design and testing would be needed to develop audio description, transcripts, larger text, step-free route information and safe navigation choices.

08 · Next steps

What I would validate

This prototype has not been usability tested. I would ask five people to explore one short Leicester route, explain what is an official sourced story and what a community memory would be, and show whether they know when it is safe to stop. I would also test an optional saved-story collection to see whether it supports discovery or distracts from the street.

  • Can people choose a place and switch views without getting stuck?
  • Do people know when it is safe to stop and use the phone?
  • Can people distinguish an official source from a future community memory?
  • Does optional story collecting support discovery or distract from the street?
  • Can people use the map and history panel across different access needs?

Findings would guide the next prototype iteration; no outcomes are claimed here.
