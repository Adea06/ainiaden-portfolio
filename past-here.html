THE EXPERIENCE PRINCIPLE

The physical environment is part of the interface. The screen should know when to step aside.

The design story

An interface that knows when to disappear.

Past Here is a concept for exploring local heritage through short, place-based stories. It asks a simple question: can a digital experience help people notice more of the city, while asking less of their screen?

01 · Starting point

From coursework to a focused experience

The starting point was University of Leicester CO7441 team coursework. Our group submission, Time Travelers Trials, explored creative, data-centred ways to connect students with Leicester’s heritage and culture.

I developed that shared starting point into Past Here: an individual place-based concept for discovering stories in Leicester’s streets. The interactive section above is a portfolio simulation, not a deployed mobile app.

The screens and visual materials are concept work. Historical scenes, names and quotes are illustrative prototype content, not verified archival evidence.

02 · Team coursework

What our group proposed

The submitted ideation document framed a student who was new to Leicester and wanted approachable ways to build a connection to the city. The persona, Adeola Thompson, and the research framing belong to the group work; they are not research I conducted for this individual case study.

Across our concept sketch, ideation document and technical diagram, the team proposed location-based discovery trails, photo-matching and knowledge challenges, AR or story layers, points, ranks, badges, leaderboards and shared journeys. The technical framework drew on place attachment and gamification, with belonging, heritage awareness and student wellbeing as intended outcomes.

Team design qualities: emotional connection · active engagement · social relevance · inclusive access · cultural continuity.

These are group concepts and intended outcomes, not validated product features or measured results.

03 · Group journey map

Five stages, from noticing to returning

Our user journey mapped the experience as Awareness → Discovery → Engagement → Reflection → Continuation. It surfaced a different concern at each stage: notification fatigue, competing city distractions, getting stuck in a challenge, hesitation to share, and the novelty fading over time.

Awareness→Discovery→Engagement→Reflection→Continuation

The map suggested personalized nearby prompts, travel information, hints or collaboration, safer community moderation and relevant return prompts. It is a team artefact; the screen-to-street flow below is my individual response to that app-heavy engagement model.

04 · Individual design move

Keep the place-based story; change the rhythm

I kept the team’s core interest in local discovery, emotional connection and stories attached to place. For Past Here, I shifted attention away from continuous in-app challenges, rankings and social activity toward a short, optional interaction at a location, followed by a phone-down walk.

“How might a digital heritage experience help people look up from their phones and notice the stories attached to the places around them?”

This is my design question and a concept hypothesis, not a user-research finding. The phone-down direction is my individual interpretation of the group journey, not a feature claimed by the original team submission.

05 · Experience rhythm

Screen → street → screen → street

The prototype makes the physical journey visible. A person chooses a short story, gets a gentle arrival cue, looks at the place, and explores a past-and-present comparison. The interface then recedes again.

Choose a story→Walk phone-down→Arrive→Explore the place→Continue walking

The portfolio simulation replaces live location and device haptics with timed screen states and a visual pulse. This demonstrates the intended interaction without asking for location permissions.

06 · Story & trust

Show where a story comes from

The experience includes two labelled perspectives: an official record and a community memory. The example content is intentionally marked as illustrative. In a real product, each account would need a traceable source, clear attribution and appropriate consent.

This concept treats heritage storytelling as interpretation rather than a single unquestioned voice. A future design iteration could test how people understand source labels and conflicting accounts.

07 · Inclusive options

More than one way to explore

The prototype offers a text-first story path and avoids relying on audio or motion to convey meaning. The experience also respects reduced-motion preferences. Further design and testing would be needed to develop audio description, transcripts, larger text, step-free route information and safe navigation choices.

08 · Next steps

What I would validate

This portfolio version communicates the design direction, but it has not been usability tested. The next step would be to test the core flow with people who explore Leicester, including residents and visitors, and learn whether the phone-down moments feel safe, clear and worthwhile.

  • Do people understand when to put the phone away and when to use it?
  • Do the arrival cues feel reassuring without drawing attention away from their surroundings?
  • Can people distinguish illustrative, official and community content?
  • Do the alternative story modes work for different access needs?

Findings would guide the next prototype iteration; no outcomes are claimed here.

A real place, across time

The Guildhall has had many lives.

Start with the building itself: a real Leicester landmark, with a history tied to civic life.

c. 1390

The Great Hall was built for the Corpus Christi Guild.

1632

Part of the Guildhall became Leicester’s town library.

19th century

It also served as the city’s first police station.

1926

It opened as a public museum.

History: Historic England · list entry 1361405. These dates describe the site; the photo above dates from 2009.

Read the building

Look for the layers.

One place, changing roles

The Guildhall was more than a meeting hall. It later hosted Leicester’s corporation, a library, court rooms and police cells. Its use changed as the city changed.

For this visit, notice the timber frame and imagine the courtyard as a working civic space. The details here are based on the official heritage record, rather than an invented person’s account.
