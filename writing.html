01 · DATA · EVIDENCE · UX

How Data Science Changed How I Design

What studying Data Science taught me about evidence, uncertainty and designing with AI and data.

DATA & DESIGN · 6 MIN READ

UX was always the direction I wanted my career to take. I chose Data Science because I wanted a stronger understanding of how evidence could support design decisions — and because I was curious about what happens when human behaviour becomes data.

My MSc changed that perspective. The important lesson wasn't simply how to analyse data or build models. It was learning to question what the evidence actually represents, what it leaves out, and what I can responsibly conclude from it.

01 The question comes before the data

In my research project, I tested whether behavioural signals such as market sentiment and VIX added value beyond traditional technical indicators. Before the model ran, I had already made decisions about the data, measures, time period and prediction target.

That made me more conscious of a principle I now bring to UX: the way a problem is framed determines what the data can tell you.

UXRELEVANCE
Analytics can show me where a problem happens. They rarely explain the whole experience behind it.

A drop-off rate can identify where users leave a journey. Interviews or usability testing can help explain whether the cause is confusion, trust, accessibility, motivation or something else.

I see quantitative and qualitative research as complementary: one can establish scale or pattern; the other can add context.

02 One metric can tell the wrong story

My model results were a useful reminder that “better” depends on what you measure. Adding behavioural features improved some measures, but not all — so I couldn't reduce the result to a simple “the model got better” story.

UXRELEVANCE
Optimising the easiest metric is not the same as improving the experience.

For a product, completion rate might improve while errors, accessibility, comprehension or trust get worse. I want to define success before choosing what to optimise — and keep secondary signals visible.[1]

03 Good evidence includes uncertainty

My research project supported a narrower conclusion than a headline claim that “behavioural indicators improve financial prediction.” Behavioural variables added value for directional prediction, but their contribution to exact return magnitude was marginal and model-dependent.

WHAT I COULD RESPONSIBLY CONCLUDETechnical indicators remained the dominant signal; sentiment and VIX provided additional, but secondary, information.

The distinction matters because useful evidence does not have to produce a definitive answer.

UXRELEVANCE
Uncertainty should shape the experience, not disappear behind it.

When evidence is incomplete or a finding is uncertain, the design should communicate that clearly rather than create a false sense of confidence. Good UX makes limitations understandable and helps people make informed decisions.

04 The interface changes how data is understood

For my Information Visualisation work, I redesigned an investor dashboard around what a mid-term investor actually needed to understand — rather than simply exposing everything the model could output.

MODEL OUTPUTProbability · VIX · RSI · MACD · sentiment · feature importance
DESIGN QUESTIONWhat does the user need to understand, and what should they do with that information?
UXRELEVANCE
UX is also about interpreting model output responsibly.

Hierarchy, language, cognitive load and visual emphasis can make an uncertain prediction feel more certain than it is. The interface is part of the model-to-human communication layer.

05 Data should inform the decision, not make it

I now think about the relationship between analytics, research and experimentation as a loop: observe → investigate → prototype → test → learn.[1]

For example, if analytics show high form abandonment, usability research might reveal that users do not understand why information is required. That gives me a testable hypothesis rather than an assumption.

Where appropriate, I can then compare completion with errors, abandonment and time-on-task — looking for secondary effects rather than treating one number as the answer.[3]

UXRELEVANCE
The goal isn't to make the data the decision-maker.

It is to use evidence to make my reasoning more explicit, testable and open to being changed.

06 What the data still cannot tell me

Research on UX and machine learning reinforced another lesson for me: designers do not need to become ML specialists to work effectively with AI. They do need enough technical understanding to question outputs, collaborate with technical teams and keep the user's context in view.[2]

Similarly, more sophisticated behavioural measurement does not automatically make research complete or representative. It still matters whose behaviour is being measured, in what context, and what is missing.

PSYCHOLOGYWhy?

What might explain the behaviour?

DATA SCIENCEEvidence

What supports the interpretation?

UXAction

07 How I design differently now

Data Science hasn't changed why I want to design. It has changed the questions I bring to a design decision:

01What does success actually mean?
02Am I measuring the right thing?
03What evidence is missing?
04Would another measure tell a different story?
05Does the finding hold outside the ideal scenario?
06How confident should I be?
07What still needs to be understood from a person?

That mindset is what I now bring to UX: enough technical understanding to question the system, enough research discipline to understand the person, and enough design judgement to decide what should happen next.

Data Science hasn't changed why I want to design. It has changed the evidence I bring to the decisions I make.

02 · AI · RESEARCH · HUMAN JUDGEMENT

Using AI Without Letting It Design for Me

How researching power and bias in AI policy changed the way I use AI as a designer.

AI & DESIGN · 6 MIN READ

I use AI regularly — to explore ideas, work through technical problems, challenge my thinking and speed up parts of my design process. I’m not particularly interested in being either for or against it.

I’m more interested in the line between using AI to support human understanding and using it to replace the process through which that understanding was created.

01 My relationship with AI started with research

Before my MSc and before building my UX portfolio, I worked as a Research Assistant with Dr Inga Ulnicane at De Montfort University. That work contributed to our published research on framing bias in AI policy.[1]

What stayed with me was not simply the topic of AI bias, but the importance of how a problem is framed. A technical framing might treat bias as something technology can detect or correct. A wider social framing also considers context, structural inequality and power.

The same principle applies to UX. If I frame a problem too narrowly, I can optimise the thing I can measure without understanding the experience behind it.

THE SAME OBSERVATION CAN LEAD TO DIFFERENT DESIGN QUESTIONS Users aren't clicking “Continue”.
FRAME A“The button isn't getting enough engagement.”→ Change colour, position or prominence.
FRAME B“Users don't understand what happens after they continue.”→ Investigate language, expectations and the journey.

The observation hasn't changed. The problem definition has — and so has the solution.

02 Faster insight isn't necessarily deeper insight

Recently, I contacted a small UX research company in Leicester whose work combined psychology, behavioural data and eye-tracking. I was interested in gaining experience with them.

The founder explained that some of the eye-tracking research work they previously relied on had been lost to AI, leaving less work available. I’ve kept the company anonymous; what mattered to me was what the conversation represented.

I had been thinking about how AI could support UX research. Here was a real example of it potentially substituting for part of that research.

There are good reasons to use AI. It can process information, organise research, identify patterns and generate alternatives quickly. Research with UX professionals also highlights both these opportunities and concerns around human agency, skills and changing professional roles.[2]

But speed and understanding are not the same measure of success.

IMAGE PLACEHOLDER · FASTER ≠ DEEPER
FASTERGenerate · Summarise · Simulate · Pattern-match
DEEPERObserve · Ask why · Hear contradictions · Understand context

Analytics might show that someone clicked the wrong button five times. Eye tracking might show where they looked. A model might identify the same behaviour across thousands of users. AI might summarise all of it beautifully.

But I may still need to ask: what did that person think was going to happen when they clicked?

03 What if the friction is where the insight lives?

Product design spends a lot of time removing friction. Usually, that is a good thing. AI can remove friction from the design process too.

Generating a persona is faster than recruiting participants. Asking AI to critique an interface is faster than organising usability testing. Summarising interviews is faster than sitting with contradictory responses and working through what they might mean.

But some of that friction is where understanding happens.

RESEARCHFriction isn't automatically a problem.

Watching someone struggle with something I thought was obvious is not bad data. Five users contradicting one another does not necessarily mean the findings need to be cleaned into one convenient insight.

Those moments can reveal assumptions, edge cases and differences in context that a neat summary might hide.

04 AI can expand the possibilities. I still own the decision.

This is the principle I’m trying to build into my own practice:

AI can expand the space of possibilities. It doesn't remove my responsibility for choosing between them.

If AI generates five interface ideas, I still need to understand which one addresses the user's problem. If it summarises research, I need to check whether it has flattened an important contradiction. If it identifies a behavioural pattern, I still need to ask what might explain it.

When an output sounds convincing, I want to ask whose perspective might be missing, what assumptions sit behind it, and whether I’m treating generated content as evidence when it isn't.

05 Data Science makes me more interested in AI — and more cautious

Studying Data Science has made me appreciate how powerful machine learning can be. It has also taught me how dependent an output is on the data, assumptions, targets and measures behind it.

That gives me a familiar set of questions when AI enters my design process:

01What information went in?
02What's missing?
03Whose behaviour is represented?
04What assumptions are being made?
05What are we actually optimising for?

The last question matters especially in UX: are we measuring the thing we care about, or simply the thing that is easiest to measure?

06 Maybe the designer becomes more important when designing becomes easier

If almost anybody can generate a polished interface in minutes, producing the interface itself becomes less distinctive. The harder questions become:

WHY?Why this problem?

What are we actually trying to solve?

WHO?Whose perspective is missing?

Who is represented — and who isn't?

WHAT IF?What happens when it gets it wrong?

Who carries the consequence?

That is why I don’t see my Psychology, Data Science, AI research and UX backgrounds as competing with one another.

Psychology makes me interested in the person behind the behaviour. Data Science gives me ways to identify patterns and question evidence. AI research taught me to look beyond purely technical solutions towards context, framing and power. UX is where I have to decide what to do with all of that.

07 I'm not trying to design without AI

I’m trying to become better at designing with it.

Some research tasks should become faster. Some may eventually be automated entirely. AI will also enable forms of research and design that weren't previously practical.

I’m interested in all of that. I just don’t want faster answers to make me less curious about how those answers were produced, whose experiences they represent, or what might have disappeared along the way.

AI can help me understand more. I just don't want it to stop me from asking people.
