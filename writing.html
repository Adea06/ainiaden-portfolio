How Data Science Changed How I Design

What studying Data Science taught me about evidence, uncertainty and understanding users.

DATA & DESIGN · 8 MIN READ

UX was always the direction I wanted my career to take. Choosing to study Data Science wasn’t a detour from that. I chose it because I could see the value that data collection, analysis and structured problem-solving could bring to understanding users and making better design decisions.

What I didn’t expect was how much studying Data Science would change the way I think about evidence itself.

I started my MSc thinking that more data would give me clearer answers. My dissertation challenged that assumption. I investigated whether behavioural finance indicators, including market sentiment and the VIX, could improve financial asset prediction beyond traditional technical indicators. What became interesting wasn’t simply whether a model improved, but what “better” actually meant.

A model could improve and still be unreliable. One metric could show progress while another exposed a weakness. Something that worked in one market might behave differently in another.

It made me realise that being data-informed isn’t simply about having more numbers. It’s about understanding what those numbers represent, what they leave out and whether they’re actually useful to the person you’re designing for.

UX RELEVANCEThe same evidence can lead to different design decisions depending on what you choose to measure and what you still need to understand.

That is why I now think of data and user research as complementary rather than competing approaches.

01 More data doesn’t mean more understanding

One of the biggest lessons I’ve taken from Data Science is that having more data doesn’t automatically mean understanding the problem.

In my dissertation, choices around sentiment, technical indicators, time periods and prediction targets influenced what my models could learn. Data doesn’t arrive without context. We make decisions about what to collect, include and measure.

DATA SCIENCEMore observations ≠ more understanding

The measurement choices shape what the analysis can tell us.

UX has the same challenge. Analytics might tell me exactly where people leave a registration journey, but not necessarily why. An interview or usability test could reveal that users don’t understand why information is being requested, or that they’re overlooking something I assumed was obvious.

UX RELEVANCEQualitative and quantitative research are different pieces of the same story.

If three people struggle with an interaction, behavioural data can help determine whether that pattern exists across hundreds or thousands of sessions. If analytics reveal a pattern, research can help explain it.

02 The metric changes the story

In my LightGBM classification work, F1 improved from Stage 1 to Stage 2 in both markets. That could suggest that adding behavioural indicators improved performance. But F1 alone wasn’t the full picture.

Looking at one metric alone would have given me a very different interpretation of the model. That matters because choosing a metric also means deciding what kind of success or failure matters.

UX RELEVANCESuccess metrics should reflect the experience you actually want to improve.

If a redesigned checkout increases completion, I would still want to know what happened to errors, time-on-task, accessibility and understanding.

King, Churchill and Tan’s Designing with Data reinforced this for me: define what you expect a change to improve, then look for evidence that tests that expectation.

03 Uncertainty isn’t something to hide

My dissertation made me more comfortable with uncertain results. Behavioural features were more useful for improving aspects of directional prediction than accurately predicting the magnitude of future returns.

WHAT I COULD RESPONSIBLY CONCLUDEBehavioural variables provided evidence-backed value for market direction, but only marginal, model-dependent value for exact return magnitude.

Technical indicators remained the dominant signal; sentiment and VIX were secondary but genuine contributors.

UX RELEVANCEResearch doesn’t always produce a clean yes or no.

An A/B test might improve one metric but not another. Usability testing might identify a problem without immediately telling me how to solve it. Communicating the limitation is part of communicating the finding.

04 A dashboard is an interpretation, not a data dump

My Information Visualisation module brought another part of this together. You can have completely accurate data and still communicate it badly.

BEFOREModel output

Technical indicators, model output and limited behavioural context.

AFTERInvestor decision support

Signal, probability, market volatility, sentiment, technical context and explanation of why the signal was produced.

UX RELEVANCEThe challenge wasn’t displaying everything the model knew. It was deciding what the investor actually needed to know.

That changed the question from “What data can I display?” to “What does this person need to understand or do?”

05 Designing with data, not being designed by it

Behavioural data can identify a pattern. User research can help explain it. A prototype can turn that understanding into a possible solution. Experimentation can then test whether the solution actually improves the experience.

UX RELEVANCEData should inform the design decision, not make it.

A redesign could increase completion while making users more confused or uncomfortable. A statistically successful optimisation isn’t automatically a good user experience.

06 What the data can’t tell me

Analytics might tell me that someone abandoned a journey. Click data might tell me exactly what they did before leaving. Neither can completely explain their frustration, hesitation, expectations or circumstances at that moment.

PSYCHOLOGYWhy?

Why are people behaving this way?

DATA SCIENCEEvidence

UXAction

What should I do with that understanding?

UX RELEVANCEBetter measurement doesn’t remove the need for human context.

That is where my Psychology background connects everything.

07 How I design differently now

The biggest thing Data Science has given me isn’t simply the ability to use Python or train a machine-learning model. It’s changed the questions I ask before making a design decision.

01What does success actually mean?
02Am I measuring the right thing?
03What information am I missing?
04Would another measure tell a different story?
05Does this work outside the ideal scenario?
06How confident should I actually be?
07What can the data tell me, and what do I still need to ask a person?
Data Science hasn’t changed why I want to design. It’s changed the evidence I bring to the decisions I make.

Using AI Without Letting It Design for Me

Researching AI bias changed the way I think about a deceptively simple question: when is AI helping me understand a problem, and when is it quietly defining the problem for me?

AI & DESIGN · 5 MIN READ

My interest in this started before UX. As a Research Assistant with Dr Inga Ulnicane, I contributed to research examining how AI policy frames bias. One useful distinction was between a technical framing, where bias is treated primarily as something technology can detect or correct, and a broader social framing that considers context, power and structural inequality.²

That idea stayed with me because the way a problem is framed changes the solutions that seem reasonable.

From observation to problem

Imagine analytics show that users repeatedly abandon a form. It is possible to frame that as an engagement problem and immediately optimise the button. It is also possible to ask whether users understand why the information is required, whether the language matches their expectations, or whether the journey is creating uncertainty.

The observation is the same. The problem definition is different. And therefore the design work is different.

That is also why I am cautious about using AI to generate personas, synthesise research or critique interfaces without checking the underlying evidence. AI can make an output look coherent very quickly, but coherence is not the same as understanding. Research with 20 UX professionals found that designers viewed GenAI largely as an assistive tool while also raising concerns around human agency, skill degradation and changing professional roles.³

How this affects my practice

I use AI to explore possibilities, organise information, generate alternatives and challenge my thinking. I remain responsible for defining the problem, judging evidence, considering missing perspectives, accessibility and making the final decision.

I apply that principle in projects such as my SST Workflow Redesign: the interface should come from understanding the real workflow and its constraints, not from asking AI to invent a plausible-looking workflow.

I want AI to increase the range and speed of my thinking without outsourcing the judgement that makes the work mine.
