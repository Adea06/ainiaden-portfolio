How Data Science Changed How I Design

What studying Data Science taught me about evidence, uncertainty and understanding users.

DATA & DESIGN · 8 MIN READ

UX was always the direction I wanted my career to take. Choosing to study Data Science wasn’t a detour from that, or because I thought the two disciplines were the same.

I chose it because I could see the value that data collection, analysis and structured problem-solving could bring to understanding users and making better design decisions.

What I didn’t expect was how much studying Data Science would change the way I think about evidence itself.

I started my MSc thinking that more data would give me clearer answers. My dissertation challenged that assumption.

I investigated whether behavioural finance indicators, including market sentiment and the VIX, could improve financial asset prediction beyond traditional technical indicators. What became interesting wasn’t simply whether a model improved, but what “better” actually meant.

A model could improve and still be unreliable. One metric could show progress while another exposed a weakness. Something that worked in one market might behave differently in another.

It made me realise that being data-informed isn’t simply about having more numbers. It’s about understanding what those numbers represent, what they leave out and whether they’re actually useful to the person you’re designing for.

01 More data doesn’t mean more understanding

One of the biggest lessons I’ve taken from Data Science is that having more data doesn’t automatically mean understanding the problem.

In my dissertation, choices around sentiment, technical indicators, time periods and prediction targets influenced what my models could learn. Data doesn’t arrive without context. We make decisions about what to collect, include and measure.

UX RELEVANCEMore data does not automatically mean more understanding.

Imagine analytics show that users regularly abandon a registration journey at the same stage. Thousands of sessions might tell me exactly where people leave, but not necessarily why.

An interview or usability test could reveal that users don’t understand why information is being requested, or that they’re overlooking something I assumed was obvious.

The reverse is useful too. If three people struggle with the same interaction during usability testing, behavioural data can help determine whether the same pattern exists across hundreds or thousands of sessions.

For me, it isn’t qualitative versus quantitative research. They’re different pieces of the same story.

That matters because choosing a metric also means deciding what kind of success or failure matters.

I’ve started thinking about UX in the same way.

If a redesigned checkout increases completion, that’s useful. But what happened to errors? Time-on-task? Accessibility? Did people actually understand the process?

King, Churchill and Tan’s Designing with Data (2017) reinforced this for me through their discussion of experimentation and A/B testing.

Instead of asking:

“Do I think this design is better?”

I can ask:

“What do I expect this change to improve, and what evidence would show me whether I’m right?”

It means defining success before becoming attached to the solution.

03 Uncertainty isn’t something to hide

My dissertation also made me more comfortable with uncertain results.

Behavioural features were more useful for improving aspects of directional prediction than accurately predicting the magnitude of future returns.

WHAT I COULD RESPONSIBLY CONCLUDEBehavioural finance variables provide real, evidence-backed value for predicting market direction, but only marginal, model-dependent value for predicting exact return magnitude.

Direction prediction proved far more tractable than magnitude. Technical indicators remained the dominant signal; sentiment and VIX were consistently secondary but genuine contributors.

It would have been easy to simplify that into “behavioural indicators improve financial prediction.”

But that would claim more than the evidence supported.

The more useful question was:

What can I responsibly conclude from this?

UX RELEVANCEUncertainty isn’t something to hide.

Research doesn’t always produce a clean yes or no. An A/B test might improve one metric but not another. Usability testing might identify a problem without immediately telling me how to solve it.

Uncertainty shouldn’t be hidden just because certainty looks better in a presentation.

Sometimes communicating the limitation is part of communicating the finding.

04 A dashboard is an interpretation, not a data dump

My Information Visualisation module brought another part of this together.

You can have completely accurate data and still communicate it badly.

While developing my investor dashboard, I had to stop thinking only about what the model could output and start thinking about what a mid-term investor actually needed to understand.

WHAT THE MODEL SEESProbability · VIX · RSI · MACD · sentiment · feature importance · performance metrics
→WHAT THE USER SEES
UX RELEVANCEThe challenge wasn’t displaying everything the model knew. It was deciding what the investor actually needed to know.

That changed the question from:

What data can I display?

to:

What does this person need to understand or do?

Suddenly hierarchy, cognitive load, language and uncertainty became just as important as the numbers themselves.

A dashboard isn’t simply displaying data. It’s interpreting it for someone.

And that means the designer has responsibility for how that information is understood.

If I present an uncertain prediction as though it’s definitive, that’s a design decision too.

05 Designing with data, not being designed by it

This is where I think Data Science and UX become particularly useful together.

Behavioural data can identify a pattern. User research can help explain it. A prototype can turn that understanding into a possible solution. Experimentation can then test whether the solution actually improves the experience.

For example, analytics might show unusually high abandonment on a form. Through usability testing, I might discover that users don’t understand why certain information is required.

That gives me a hypothesis:

If I explain why the information is needed and reveal complex fields progressively, users may be more likely to complete the journey successfully.

I can prototype that idea and test it. If appropriate, I could A/B test the existing and redesigned experiences and compare completion, abandonment, errors and time-on-task.

But even then, the numbers don’t get the final word.

A redesign could increase completion while making users more confused or uncomfortable.

A statistically successful optimisation isn’t automatically a good user experience.

UX RELEVANCEData should inform the design decision, not make it.

06 What the data can’t tell me

There are still things a dataset can’t completely capture.

The person.

Analytics might tell me that someone abandoned a journey. Click data might tell me exactly what they did before leaving.

Neither can completely explain their frustration, hesitation, expectations or circumstances at that moment.

Research into machine learning and UX (Abbas, Ghauth and Ting, 2022) and eye tracking (Novák et al., 2024) shows how increasingly sophisticated methods can help us understand behavioural patterns.

But better measurement doesn’t remove the need for human context.

07 How I design differently now

The biggest thing Data Science has given me isn’t simply the ability to use Python or train a machine-learning model.

It’s changed the questions I ask before making a design decision.

01What does success actually mean?
02Am I measuring the right thing?
03What information am I missing?
04Would another measure tell a different story?
05Does this work outside the ideal scenario?
06How confident should I actually be?
07What can the data tell me, and what do I still need to ask a person?

I chose Data Science because I believed it could add another layer to how I approached UX. What I gained was more than the technical ability to analyse data or build a model.

It taught me to question what I’m measuring, recognise uncertainty, test whether findings hold outside ideal conditions and avoid claiming more than the evidence supports.

Data can help me see patterns at a scale that interviews alone cannot. User research can give me context that a dataset alone cannot.

Data Science hasn’t changed why I want to design. It’s changed the evidence I bring to the decisions I make.

Using AI Without Letting It Design for Me

Researching AI bias changed the way I think about a deceptively simple question: when is AI helping me understand a problem, and when is it quietly defining the problem for me?

AI & DESIGN · 5 MIN READ

My interest in this started before UX. As a Research Assistant with Dr Inga Ulnicane, I contributed to research examining how AI policy frames bias. One useful distinction was between a technical framing, where bias is treated primarily as something technology can detect or correct, and a broader social framing that considers context, power and structural inequality.²

That idea stayed with me because the way a problem is framed changes the solutions that seem reasonable.

From observation to problem

Imagine analytics show that users repeatedly abandon a form. It is possible to frame that as an engagement problem and immediately optimise the button. It is also possible to ask whether users understand why the information is required, whether the language matches their expectations, or whether the journey is creating uncertainty.

The observation is the same. The problem definition is different. And therefore the design work is different.

That is also why I am cautious about using AI to generate personas, synthesise research or critique interfaces without checking the underlying evidence. AI can make an output look coherent very quickly, but coherence is not the same as understanding. Research with 20 UX professionals found that designers viewed GenAI largely as an assistive tool while also raising concerns around human agency, skill degradation and changing professional roles.³

How this affects my practice

I use AI to explore possibilities, organise information, generate alternatives and challenge my thinking. I remain responsible for defining the problem, judging evidence, considering missing perspectives, accessibility and making the final decision.

I apply that principle in projects such as my SST Workflow Redesign: the interface should come from understanding the real workflow and its constraints, not from asking AI to invent a plausible-looking workflow.

I want AI to increase the range and speed of my thinking without outsourcing the judgement that makes the work mine.
