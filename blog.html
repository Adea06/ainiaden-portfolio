DATA · EVIDENCE · UX

How Data Science Changed How I Design

Studying Data Science changed less about why I want to design than about the evidence I expect to bring to a design decision.

I started my MSc thinking that more data would naturally lead to clearer answers. My dissertation challenged that assumption. While testing whether behavioural indicators such as sentiment and the VIX could improve financial prediction beyond technical indicators, I kept finding that the answer depended on what I measured, how I measured it and where I tested it.

The pattern I started noticing

A model could improve on one metric while exposing a weakness on another. A finding that appeared useful in one market did not automatically generalise elsewhere. That made me less interested in asking whether something was simply “better” and more interested in defining what better means for the person using the result.

That thinking carried directly into my Behavioural Investor Dashboard. The challenge was no longer just displaying probabilities, sentiment and technical indicators. It was deciding what a mid-term investor actually needed to understand, what uncertainty needed explaining, and which information should sit behind the main decision.

Research on data-informed design makes a similar point: experimentation is useful when the team first establishes what it expects a change to improve and then evaluates the evidence rather than relying on intuition alone.¹

What I take into UX

I now try to move through a problem with three questions: What does the evidence show? What might it be missing? What does the person still need to tell me? Analytics can reveal a pattern; interviews or usability testing can help explain it. A prototype can turn that understanding into a hypothesis, and testing can tell me whether the hypothesis holds.

Most importantly, I have become more comfortable saying when the evidence is inconclusive. I would rather communicate a useful limitation than turn an uncertain result into a confident design story.

The lesson: Data should make my design decisions more informed, not make the decisions for me.

AI · RESEARCH · HUMAN JUDGEMENT

Using AI Without Letting It Design for Me

Researching AI bias changed the way I think about a deceptively simple question: when is AI helping me understand a problem, and when is it quietly defining the problem for me?

My interest in this started before UX. As a Research Assistant with Dr Inga Ulnicane, I contributed to research examining how AI policy frames bias. One useful distinction was between a technical framing, where bias is treated primarily as something technology can detect or correct, and a broader social framing that considers context, power and structural inequality.²

That idea stayed with me because the way a problem is framed changes the solutions that seem reasonable.

From observation to problem

Imagine analytics show that users repeatedly abandon a form. It is possible to frame that as an engagement problem and immediately optimise the button. It is also possible to ask whether users understand why the information is required, whether the language matches their expectations, or whether the journey is creating uncertainty.

The observation is the same. The problem definition is different. And therefore the design work is different.

That is also why I am cautious about using AI to generate personas, synthesise research or critique interfaces without checking the underlying evidence. AI can make an output look coherent very quickly, but coherence is not the same as understanding. Research with 20 UX professionals found that designers viewed GenAI largely as an assistive tool while also raising concerns around human agency, skill degradation and changing professional roles.³

How this affects my practice

I use AI to explore possibilities, organise information, generate alternatives and challenge my thinking. I remain responsible for defining the problem, judging evidence, considering missing perspectives, accessibility and making the final decision.

I apply that principle in projects such as my SST Workflow Redesign: the interface should come from understanding the real workflow and its constraints, not from asking AI to invent a plausible-looking workflow.

The lesson: I want AI to increase the range and speed of my thinking without outsourcing the judgement that makes the work mine.

BUILDING · RETRIEVAL · PORTFOLIO UX

Building and Deploying Ask Aini

Building an AI portfolio assistant made me treat my own portfolio as a product: if a recruiter cannot find the evidence quickly, the information architecture is part of the problem.

The initial idea was simple: let a recruiter ask a natural-language question instead of searching through several case studies. But the interesting design problem was not the chatbot itself. It was deciding what the assistant should be allowed to say, what evidence it should retrieve and when it should admit that the portfolio does not contain an answer.

Designing for evidence, not just answers

I structured the knowledge around real evidence: Psychology and Data Science background, workflow redesign, dashboards, research, accessibility and technical work. I also separated completed projects from ongoing work and concepts. That distinction matters because a fluent AI response can otherwise make proposed work sound like shipped experience.

The assistant therefore has a deliberately narrow role. It should connect a recruiter’s question to a relevant project, explain the evidence concisely and point back to the case study. It should not invent clients, leadership responsibilities, research participants or outcomes.

The pattern behind the product

This project reinforced something I keep seeing in my other work: good information is not enough if the user has to work too hard to interpret or locate it. In the investor dashboard, that meant turning model outputs into a decision-support interface. Here, it means turning a portfolio into something that can be explored conversationally without losing the source evidence.

The lesson: I am increasingly interested in the layer between complex information and the person who needs to make sense of it.

CAREER · ITERATION · EVIDENCE

What Job Hunting Is Teaching Me About Becoming a Designer

Applying for UX roles has become another design problem: observe the response, identify the pattern, change the system and test again.

I initially thought a strong portfolio mainly needed to show good-looking work. Applying for roles has changed that. Across job descriptions, application tasks and feedback, I kept seeing the same expectations: research, accessibility, product thinking, collaboration and the ability to explain why a decision was made.

That changed how I describe my own projects. Instead of leading with the finished screen, I now try to show the problem, the constraints, what I contributed, what changed and what I would test next.

What changed in my portfolio

The clearest example is the SST Workflow Redesign. It is not simply a Figma screen. The useful story is the existing workflow, the people and constraints around it, the patterns found through conversations, and how those findings shaped the proposed system.

The same shift happened with the Behavioural Investor Dashboard: I now show the move from a technically capable model to an interface designed around what an investor actually needs to understand.

The lesson: A portfolio is not only a gallery of outputs. It is evidence of how I notice problems, make decisions and learn from what happens next.

CAREER · BARCELONA · LEARNING IN PUBLIC

Breaking Into UX in Barcelona

What I’m learning while preparing to relocate: treat assumptions as hypotheses, gather evidence and change the plan when the evidence changes.

I am preparing for a move to Barcelona rather than presenting myself as someone who already understands its UX market. That distinction matters. My current view comes from analysing roles, speaking with people in the industry and paying attention to what employers repeatedly ask for.

What I am looking for in the evidence

I’m tracking recurring requirements across UX and Product roles: research, accessibility, product thinking, data-informed decisions, collaboration and the ability to communicate clearly in a team. I’m also paying attention to language requirements and the difference between what a job advert says and what people working in the market describe in practice.

Rather than treating two or three conversations as representative of Barcelona, I want to use them as individual perspectives that can challenge my assumptions. The same principle I use in research applies here: an observation is useful, but its context determines how much I can conclude from it.

What I am changing

That has pushed me towards a more targeted portfolio, clearer project evidence and building relationships before I relocate. I am also treating the move itself as an iterative process: learn, adjust, test again.

The lesson: I do not need to pretend I already know the answer. I need a clear question, good evidence and the willingness to update my approach.
