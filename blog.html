RESEARCH · REFLECTION · SEPTEMBER 2026

What a workaround can tell you

When a process feels awkward, people rarely stop everything to report it. They find a way through. They keep another spreadsheet open, save a note for later, or ask the colleague who knows where to click. The task gets done, but the workaround can become so familiar that nobody notices how much effort it takes.

That is one reason I want to spend time understanding users before deciding what to redesign. A list of requested features can tell me what people think a product needs. Watching someone work through a real task can show me where they lose track of information, which steps they repeat, and what they have learned to do outside the system.

My psychology background makes me curious about those moments of hesitation. Data science gives me another way to look for patterns across a process. Neither replaces a conversation with the person doing the work. I want to bring all three together: listen carefully, look for recurring problems, and test whether a design actually makes the task easier.

I’m carrying that approach into my work on academic support workflows. The aim is not simply to put existing information on a new screen. I want to understand which details help a tutor decide what to do next, and which parts of the current process make that decision harder. My prototypes are a way to test those ideas, not proof that I have already solved the problem.

The question I’m trying to keep in mind is simple: what has someone had to invent to make this product work for them? That is often where the most useful design conversation begins.
